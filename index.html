How rare are negative and even splits among elite ultrarunners, and do world-class efforts pace more evenly? These pages explore a research dataset built from official results and lap data. Work in progress — findings are preliminary and the full analysis is not yet published.

Start here — the argument, in reading order

The paper's case, built for a skeptical reader. Each page states its own caveats. The badge is the page's number, which the cross-references between pages use; the order below is the argument's, not the number's.

  • 17How negative splits actually happen
    A verdict for every lap-resolved negative/even 24-hour split — held back, metronome, overcooked, or stop-and-recover. The 46 split 5 / 12 / 22 / 7: most are accidents.
  • 19Marathon pacing vs ultra pacing
    Verified marathon world-record halves and the published pacing literature, set against this dataset. Marathon records run within ~1% of even; elite 24-hour fields fade +14%.
  • 18Evenness inside a single career
    590 athletes compared only with themselves, which removes the ability confound. An athlete's best day is usually their most even one.
  • 28Who has done it more than once
    Every athlete with two or more negative or even races, and a permutation test on whether that clusters. 19 have done it twice; chance predicts 7.
  • 20How often anyone even tries
    Measures the attempt rather than the outcome: who ever opened conservatively? 9 of 3,264 full-day efforts — the rarity is mostly the rarity of trying.
  • 21The confounds, and a design that survives them
    Two charts that look like evidence and are not, why each fails, and a matched-pairs test that holds race and halfway distance fixed. The evener arrival wins 64% of pairs.
  • 22What the fade is actually made of
    Second-half time-loss broken into gradual drift and discrete bad patches. 62% is bad patches, and they are foreshadowed before halfway.
  • 23One athlete's attempt log
    65 self-recorded pacing attempts over 15 years, kept outside the database by design and presented as anecdote. 62% success, transferring up in distance last.
  • 26Every race in that log, measured from GPS
    The same career re-measured from the athlete's own tracks, with a terrain-adjustment toggle. 37 negative splits by the clock, 27 once the ground is levelled.
  • 27Every long run, when nobody is watching
    119 non-race runs of 25+ miles — the unlogged default, not the attempts. Faster second half on 40% before 2020, 61% after.
  • 24Heart rate behind an even pace
    Per-mile heart rate and stopped time from the American Record run. −1.6% Pa:HR decoupling over 24 hours; 8.8 minutes stopped all day.
  • 25What would actually settle this
    The discussion section: the paper's five claims, what each still rests on, and the four things the sport would have to publish or run to test them.

Race shapes — how a single race unfolds

These need lap-by-lap data, so they cover the ~140 lap-resolved events rather than the whole dataset.

  • 16Intentional vs accidental
    The metric the census runs on: seconds of intentionality — the mean gap in s/mi between what an athlete was on pace for and what they finally averaged.
  • 30Inside one discipline
    Pick a rung and see nothing else: the distance's pace signature, its foresight fan, its verdict mix, and whether its record-caliber runs open more patiently than the rest.
  • 31Intentionality across the ladder
    Every rung collapsed to one number, shortest first. The 800 m inverts; the 1500 m, mile, 5 km and 10 km sit in an unseparated plateau just under zero; and from the marathon up it climbs by a factor of six.
  • 32Smoothness — the shape of the intent
    A second axis intentionality is blind to: did the pace move in one steady arc or in lurches? Defined on ten race deciles so it measures the running and not the timing mats — with the resampling study that shows the naive version measures the mats.
  • 2Projected finish, from your pace so far
    What each point of the race projected to, given the average pace up to it. Overlay, single race, or small multiples.
  • 3Pace from here to the end
    The mirror image: average pace from each point to the finish. A negative split starts on the line and climbs above it.
  • 4Shape metrics
    First half vs second half, late surge, steadiness, and quarter-by-quarter signature curves across the full rich-lap cohort.
  • 12Pacing archetypes & race replay
    Two-dimensional archetype quadrants from go-out-hard-and-fade to held-back-kick, plus an animated replay of one race against its even-pace ghost.
