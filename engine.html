24 · The engine room — even pace, even effort?

A fair objection to everything on this site: pace is the output. An even pace could still be a desperate, ever-costlier grind — in which case the "held-back" races are just suffering arranged differently, and there is nothing to learn. Heart rate is the only effort signal we have at 24-hour scale, and it exists for exactly two of the held-back exhibits: per-mile HR from the athlete's own recording of the 2021 American Record (173.015 mi, −1.03%) and the 2021 Dome 100-mile (13:21, −3.12%). The standard endurance metric here is Pa:HR decoupling — how much the pace-per-heartbeat ratio degrades from the first half to the second. Under +5% over a few hours is textbook "well-executed aerobic effort." These two runs post −1.6% over 24 hours and −14.8% over 100 miles: the engine got more efficient as the race went on.

Chart 24.1 — The American Record, mile by mile: pace up, heart rate flat

Both series indexed to their own race mean (100 = this run's average), one axis, no tricks. Moving pace (teal, above 100 = faster than race average) climbs steadily through the day; heart rate (purple) rises barely 2% half-to-half (174 → 178 bpm). A fading runner shows the opposite geometry — pace sagging under a flat-or-rising HR. Faint lines are raw miles; bold lines are 5-mile rolling means.

Chart 24.2 — The Dome 100: negative-splitting at falling heart rate

The stranger exhibit. The last quarter was the fastest of the race (7:31/mi vs 8:16 opening) at a heart rate 17 bpm lower than the opening quarter's. That is what genuine reserve looks like: the early restraint was so deep that the fast close cost less than the easy start. Same athlete, same year as the AR.

Chart 24.3 — Stopped time: 8.8 minutes in 24 hours

Cumulative stopped time through the AR run (from the same recording; the official timing feed independently agrees — its longest gap between crossings is 2.9 minutes). Recall from Exploration 22 that the median elite fade is 62% bad patches: this run simply never entered that regime. Total non-moving time across a full day: less than one lap of a track takes most runners.

Reading it honestly

The held-back races were not evenly-distributed suffering — they were physiologically comfortable until the end. Negative decoupling over these durations means the second half was run at a better pace-per-heartbeat than the first: the "cost" of the early restraint was repaid with interest, exactly as the strategy intends. This is the mechanism the aggregate charts can't see: the plan works (when it works) by keeping the engine below its breaking point through the hours where everyone else buys their later collapse.
Caveats — this is the N=1 page's little sibling, and inherits every one of its warnings. Two runs, one athlete, self-recorded on a consumer device (HR strap quality, caffeine, indoor temperature, and circadian effects all move HR independent of effort — though most of those push HR up late, which would mask, not fake, the effect shown). HR is a proxy for cardiovascular strain, not glycogen or muscle damage. What this page establishes is existence, not expectation: at least one athlete has run a 24-hour American Record with essentially zero aerobic decoupling — so "even pace over a day is physiologically impossible without a hidden grind" is falsified as a universal. Whether it is reproducible is the research question.
