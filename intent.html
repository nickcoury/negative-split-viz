Exploration 16 · Intentional vs accidental negative splits

A negative split can happen two very different ways. Intentional: hold back on purpose — the foresight line (what you're on pace for) sits below your eventual average for most of the race and only rises to meet it late. Accidental: go out ahead of final pace, something goes wrong, and the recovery happens to make the second half faster. Identical sign of split_pct, different class of performance.
All three lines are put on one scale: x = % of race completed, y = % ahead of your own final average pace (+ = on pace for a better result than you got, − = conservative). The metric is the area between each line and zero, split above/below: score = (area below − area above) / total, in [−1, +1]. It is computed for all three lines so they can be compared (chart 16.5), but only the foresight line is used to call a performance intentional or accidental: Hindsight is close to a mirror of foresight and instantaneous pace barely varies, so neither carries an independent verdict.

Seconds of intentionality — the headline number on this page. The normalised score above has the right shape but throws away magnitude and saturates hard (most 24-hour races sit at exactly −1.00), so a runner who went out 4 s/mi hot scores the same as one who went out 280. This is the same integral with a unit on it: take the foresight line, measure its distance from your own final average pace in seconds per mile, and average that over the race with x normalised to [0, 1] — so race length drops out and a 24-hour effort and a 100-mile effort are measured the same way. intentionality = ∫₀¹ (final average pace − pace so far) dx
Negative = you ran conservative of the pace you ended up averaging — held back early, looks intentional. Positive = you ran ahead of it — went out at a pace you couldn't hold, looks accidental. Open ten seconds per mile down on your eventual average and close the gap steadily and you read about −5 s/mi: half the peak, because that is the area under a line falling from 10 to 0. Real races aren't linear, which is the point of integrating rather than just reading the start.

Chart 16.1 — One race, all three lines

Only the foresight line is scored — it is the average pace so far, so its area is the metric. Don't read the verdict off the instantaneous line: segment pace is a deviation from the race average, so its area above and below is zero for everyone by construction, and a conservative start shows up there as a short deep dip paid back by a long shallow rise. That rise looks like the bigger area and isn't.

Chart 16.2 — The same race, in seconds of intentionality

The foresight line again, now as seconds per mile against your own final average pace. The shaded areas are the two halves of the integral; the number is their net, per mile of racing. Below the line = conservative · above = ahead of final pace. The early spike is real and it counts: at one hour in, every 24-hour runner is on pace for far more than they will get.

Chart 16.3 — Does intentionality separate the classes?

Each point is one performance: x = seconds of intentionality (left = held back / intentional, right = went out ahead / accidental), y = split % (below 0 = negative split). If the hypothesis holds, negative splits should spread across x rather than cluster — the ones on the left held back on purpose, the ones on the right didn't.

Chart 16.4 — Distribution of intentionality

Where this cohort actually sits. Unlike the normalised score, this metric doesn't saturate, so the tail of runners who went out enormously hot stays visible instead of piling up at −1.

Chart 16.5 — Which line is the better discriminator?

The normalised [−1, +1] score computed from each of the three lines. A useful discriminator should spread out; one that hugs zero carries no signal. Note this score's sign is the OPPOSITE of the seconds metric's: here + = conservative.
