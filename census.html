17 · The Census — how negative splits actually happen

In the marathon, the negative split is orthodoxy: hold back, finish faster, run your best. In 24-hour racing the same idea is nearly extinct in practice. This page is a complete accounting of every negative and even 24-hour split in the dataset — not just how many there are, but how each one happened. The claim it examines: when a negative split shows up in a 24-hour race, it is usually an accident — the residue of a blow-up or a long stop — and the deliberate ones can be counted on one hand.

The funnel

Start from every classified 24-hour half-split and keep narrowing: how many split negative or even, how many of those we hold full lap data for, and how many of those actually held back on purpose (foresight intentionality ≤ −1 s/mi — see Exploration 16).

The four ways a negative split happens

Every lap-resolved negative/even splitter gets a verdict from two measurements: seconds of intentionality (was the athlete ever ahead of the pace they finally averaged?) and the longest gap between timing crossings against their own feed's median gap (did a long stop sink the average mid-race?). Definitions are computed in build_viz.py, next to the metric itself.

Each point is one lap-resolved negative/even 24-hour split. x = seconds of intentionality (left of zero = never ran ahead of their own final average — the planned ones), y = split %. Marker size tracks final distance; ◆ marks a detected stop. The left edge is nearly empty — that emptiness is the finding.

The census — all 46, verdict by verdict

Longest stop is shown with the feed's own median gap beside it — on an hourly-sampled feed (median ≈ 60 min) a sub-hour stop is invisible, so stop verdicts there under-fire, never over-fire. 27 more negative/even splitters are still halves-only (no lap data yet) and cannot be given a verdict; several of those are likely artefacts of DUV's snap-to-lap bias (see methodology).

Where they happen

Chart 17.3 — Rarity is universal; championships tilt slightly even

Share of 24-hour halves splitting negative-or-even (dark) and near-even, to +3% (light), by championship tier. Every tier lives around 1% — but world championships carry nearly twice the near-even share of open races, consistent with the lap-resolved picture: the same French venue produced seven near-even runs in a Worlds field and none in six domestic fields.

Chart 17.4 — And it isn't becoming a technique

Negative+even rate by decade. If the marathon's pacing orthodoxy were migrating into 24-hour racing, this line would climb. It doesn't: the rate has wobbled around 0.5–1.3% for forty years. Rates for men (1.06%) and women (1.02%) are indistinguishable.

The embedded hundred — where holding back already exists

Run the same census on the 100-mile efforts inside 24-hour races (first 50 miles vs second 50; genuine 100-mile performances only — the ≥18h "slow shuffle" rows are excluded) and the picture changes character. The negative/even rate is ~2.5× the 24-hour rate, and the verdict mix flips: held-back is one in five here, not one in nine — and there are no stop-artefacts at all once the census is clipped to the effort itself.

Chart 17.5 — Verdict mix: whole day vs embedded hundred

Share of each verdict among lap-resolved negative/even splits. The held-back hundred is not exotic — Sorokin ran −0.45% through 100 miles during his world-record 24-hour, and Herron, Bereznowska and Ishikawa all appear. The skill exists in the sport and is already exercised over the first hundred miles of great 24-hour runs; what is missing is carrying it to the finish. That reframes the research question from "can anyone do this?" to "why does it stop at mile 100?"

Who shows up — the championship effect, tested and dissolved

Chart 17.6 — At fixed performance, tier doesn't matter

Chart 17.3 shows world championships carry ~2× the near-even share of open races. Is that athletes pacing differently at championships, or just who shows up? Two controls say composition: the same athlete at a championship vs an open race within two years (… pairs) paces essentially identically (median difference …pp; more even at the championship only …% of the time) — and among 200km+ finishers, the mean split is ~+10–11% in every tier. The near-even excess at Worlds is the concentration of good athletes, not a championship pacing effect. (This dissolves an earlier working hypothesis of this project — the six French domestic fields with zero negative splits are simply small fields of the relevant caliber, not evidence of a cultural difference.)

What this says

Negative splits are not a strategy in 24-hour racing today — they are mostly debris. Of 46 lap-resolved negative/even splits, 29 (63%) are accidents: 22 athletes went out ahead of the pace they could hold and happened to close, and 7 had the split handed to them by a multi-hour stop. Twelve more were metronomes — superbly even championship runs that drifted under the wire without ever holding back. Just 5 athletes ran the marathoner's race: conservative of their own final average from the start, closing over the day — about 1 in 1,400 of all classified 24-hour halves.
The deliberate five are not a random five. They include the American record (Coury, Desert Solstice 2021, −1.03%) and a top-ten women's run at the 2025 Worlds built the same way (Frederick, −0.78%), plus three runs at Tapiola on lap-complete feeds. Held-back races and stop-artefacts sit at opposite ends of every measurement we have — intentionality, stop length, outcome — which is why the census can tell them apart mechanically.
Caveats. Verdicts exist only where laps exist, and lap-resolved events skew toward venues whose timing survived — the 27 unresolved splitters could shift the verdict counts, though several are probably DUV lap-snap artefacts and will dissolve on resolution. Stop detection on hourly-sampled feeds (both Albi championships) misses stops shorter than the sampling interval, so stop-recover is a floor there. The marathon contrast in the intro is quantified with external sources on Exploration 19. And before reading rarity as a verdict on the strategy, see Exploration 20: the rarity of the outcome turns out to be mostly the rarity of the attempt.
