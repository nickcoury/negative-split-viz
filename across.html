Exploration 31 · Intentionality across the ladder

The companion to Exploration 30, which zooms into one rung. Here every chart collapses each race distance to one number or one line, so the rungs can be laid side by side and the question becomes how does pacing intent change as a race gets longer. Metric throughout: seconds of intentionality — the mean gap in s/mi between what an athlete was on pace for at each point and what they finally averaged. Negative = held back early; positive = went out ahead of a pace they could not hold. Colour is the rung, cool for short and warm for long, and the ordering is always the ladder's.

Three things this page cannot separate, and every reading has to carry them. (1) Format is confounded with duration below the 10 km — every 800 m, 1500 m, mile and 5 km row is a championship or invitational TRACK race, sit-and-kick and won on the last lap, while the 10 km, half and marathon rows are paced road time trials. The size of that is measured, not feared: at one distance, a championship round paces about 2.8 pp more negative than a paced Diamond League race (docs/AGENT_ONBOARDING.md). (2) The selection rule differs by rung. Eight rungs take everything inside 110% of the world record; the marathon and 100 km are hand-set (2:12/2:25, 7:00/8:00); the 100-mile and 24-hour are absolute (finish inside a day, cover a hundred miles). A number read across the panels is read across three selection rules. (3) The shape resolution differs by two orders of magnitude — ten mats on a marathon against four hundred lap crossings in a 24-hour race. What that costs the intentionality integral is measured and small (median 0.63 s/mi, verdict flips on 0 of 3,457 resampled ultras); what it costs the roughness metric is neither, which is why smoothness gets its own page and its own caveats.

Controls

31.1 — The intentionality ladder

Median seconds of intentionality per rung, shortest first, with a bootstrap 95% interval. The interval resamples EDITIONS, not performances — sixty runners in one race are not sixty independent observations, and treating them as such makes an interval roughly half as wide as it should be (analysis/rate_fragility.py measured an intra-class correlation of 0.117 and a design effect of 4.16 on the 10 km rung). The box behind each point is the interquartile range of the performances themselves, which is far wider and is the honest picture of the spread.

31.2 — How often anyone holds back at all

The share of each rung's performances that open conservatively enough to be called deliberate — intent_s ≤ −1 s/mi, the census threshold. A rate is a count on one side of a fixed line, so it moves much faster than a median does under a field-wide shift; the median in 31.1 is the number to trust and this is the one that is easy to picture.

31.3 — World-class against the rest, on every rung

The question this page exists for. Each rung's performances are split at a caliber line — the closest decile to the reference mark against everyone else — and the two medians are drawn as a dumbbell. A line pointing left means the better performances opened more conservatively. The reference is the world record on eight rungs and this dataset's best on the two longest, so the 100-mile and 24-hour dumbbells measure a slightly different thing from the other seven.

31.4 — Every rung's pace signature on one grid

The median instantaneous pace over ten race deciles, one line per rung, as a percentage of each athlete's own average — up is faster. This is the ladder's shape catalogue: a tactical track race sags in the middle and kicks; a road race drifts down; a 24-hour race opens far too fast, craters, and rallies. Ten deciles at every distance, so the comparison is not a comparison of how densely the courses are matted.

31.5 — The verdict mix, rung by rung

The census taxonomy stacked per rung. stop & recover is only ever offered to a rung whose feed is dense enough to see a stop: at a 5 km cadence there is no gap long enough to trip the detector, and 802 of 804 verdict flips in the resampling study were the detector going blind rather than the runner behaving differently — so those rungs are drawn as a three-way mix and the bar is marked.

31.6 — Two metrics, ten rungs

Each rung as one point: x = median split % (the halves comparison the paper's headline rests on), y = median seconds of intentionality (the whole-race integral). If the two said the same thing the rungs would fall on a line. Where a rung sits off it, the two halves of the race agree about something the rest of the race does not.

31.7 — Against duration itself

The continuous version of 31.1: x = the rung's median race duration on a log scale, from under two minutes to a full day, y = median intentionality. Duration rather than distance because that is the axis the physiology runs on. Point size is the number of performances.

31.8 — Does the split tell you what the pacing was?

Per rung, the rank correlation between a performance's split % and its seconds of intentionality. Near −1 the two metrics are one metric and the cheap one (two checkpoints) carries the expensive one's information. Away from it, a split is not a pacing plan — and the gap is where the lap data earns its keep.

31.9 — The full distribution, not just the middle

A violin per rung. The medians in 31.1 hide how differently shaped these populations are: some rungs are a tight band and some are a long tail of blow-ups with a hard floor at the conservative end.

31.10 — The caliber gradient, rung by rung

31.3 in more resolution: each rung's median intentionality in five caliber quintiles, best on the left. A rung whose line falls to the left is one where the great performances are the patient ones. Lines are offset vertically by rung so they do not overplot; read the SHAPE of each, not its height.
