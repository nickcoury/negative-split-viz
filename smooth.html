Exploration 32 · Smoothness — the shape of the intent, not its direction

Seconds of intentionality answers one question and is blind to another. It is an integral: which side of their own final average pace did this athlete spend the race on. Two runners can post the same number and have run visibly different races — one drifting down in a single steady line, the other alternating surges and sags around the same drift; one climbing monotonically, the other running a U. This page is an attempt to define the missing axis and then to test whether the definition is worth having.

The definition, and the one constraint that shaped it. The obvious measure of erraticness is the standard deviation of the checkpoint-to-checkpoint pace — and it is a measurement of the timing system, not of the running. Running pace is close to a 1/f process: the finer you sample it, the more variance you find, without bound. Measured here on 4,004 lap-complete ultras, resampling the same performances down to a marathon's ten mats halves that statistic (0.52× the full-resolution value). A cross-discipline smoothness league table built on it would rank a marathon as glassy and a track ultra as chaotic for no reason but the mats.
So every performance here is measured on one fixed grid of ten race deciles, whatever its native cadence. A decile is 10% of the race at every distance, so the frequencies only a lap list can see are excluded from the numerator and the denominator instead of inflating one of them. The ten decile paces are expressed as a percentage of that athlete's own average — up is faster — and a smooth trend (a straight line plus one bend, in race progress) is fitted to them. What comes out:

The five numbers

NameWhat it isReads as
rough RMS of what is left after the smooth trend, in percentage points of the athlete's own pace. THE headline. 0 = the pace never left its own trend. Direction-free and magnitude-free: a steady 12-point fade and a steady 12-point progression both score 0.
prog Rank correlation between position in the race and pace at that position. +1 = every tenth faster than the last, a monotone progression. −1 = a monotone fade. 0 = no trend, or a trend that turns around.
quad_r2 Share of the decile variance the smooth trend explains, in [0, 1]. Was this shape a plan or a scramble? High = the whole race is one arc. Careful: a metronome can score low simply because it has almost no variance for a trend to explain — read it beside sd.
curv The bend, end-to-middle, in percentage points. Positive = a SAG: fast start, slow middle, fast finish. Negative = a BULGE: slow at both ends, quickest through the middle.
rev How many times the tenth-to-tenth direction changes, 0 to 8. 0 = the pace only ever moved one way. The blunt, assumption-free version of rough.

These are not all equally safe to compare across distances, and the page says which are. A decile grid is exact whenever the feed's marks land on the decile boundaries — an even cadence of 10 or 50 checkpoints reproduces the full-resolution answer to three decimals — and blurs across them when they do not. A marathon's 5 km mats on a 42.195 km course line up with nothing. Chart 32.6 is that measurement, and its conclusion is that prog and quad_r2 are cross-rung statistics while rough and curv may only be compared within a cadence.

Where this definition is still soft

Ten bins is a choice, and it is the tightest constraint on the whole page. It was picked because it is the coarsest cadence a major rung publishes — a marathon's ten mats, a 10,000 m's ten kilometre marks — so it is the finest grid every rung can support. That also means an 800 m timed every 100 m has eight marks and cannot support it at all: its deciles are interpolated and its roughness is biased toward zero by 2.67 pp (measured). The 800 m is excluded by default here rather than drawn with an asterisk.

A quadratic is a choice too. Fitting only a line plus one bend means a genuine three-phase race — out hard, settle, kick — is charged as roughness rather than as shape. That is deliberate, because a higher-order fit would start absorbing the very wobble the metric exists to count, and there is no principled place to stop. rev is there as the check: where rough and rev disagree about a rung, the fit is doing something arguable.

Roughness is not the same as bad running, and this page does not claim it is. Chart 32.7 asks whether the better performances are smoother, and the honest reading of it is per-rung and correlational: a great performance is run by a great athlete, and nothing here separates the pacing from the runner. The within-athlete version of that question — the design that removes the ability confound — is Exploration 18, and it has not yet been run on these metrics.

And a stop is invisible at this grid. A ten-minute stop inside a 24-hour race moves one decile by a fraction of a point; the census's stop_recover verdict finds it by looking at the raw gaps between crossings and cannot be reconstructed from ten bins. Roughness and the stop detector are answering different questions on purpose.
