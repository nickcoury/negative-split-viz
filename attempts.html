20 · Nobody is shooting threes

Before the three-point revolution, basketball's data "showed" the long shot didn't pay — because the players taking it hadn't spent years practicing it. An outcome statistic says nothing about a strategy nobody attempts. So this page measures attempts, not outcomes: across every full-day, lap-resolved elite 24-hour effort in the dataset, who ever opened their first six hours at or below the pace they would finally average? That is the minimum entry ticket to a planned negative split — and almost nobody has ever bought one.

Chart 20.1 — The opening-surplus distribution, and its empty left tail

Each full-day effort's first-6-hour pace, as % above the pace the athlete finally averaged over 24 hours. The field lives at +15 to +35% — everyone banks. At-or-below zero — the shaded region — there are nine runs, ever. This is what "the strategy is untested" looks like as a histogram.

The nine attempts on record — and what they cost

Every one of them, with outcome. Six of nine landed a negative or even split (five negative outright). And the failures are not cautionary tales: the worst outcome among the nine is a +6.5% split — half the fade of the median elite effort (+14%). On this evidence, attempting costs little; it just isn't attempted.

Reading it honestly

0.3% attempt rate is the finding that reframes every other chart. "Negative splits don't win 24-hour races" is a claim about nine data points, six of which landed negative or even — including an American record and a top-ten Worlds run. The other 3,255 elite efforts tell you nothing about the strategy, because they never tried it. Rarity of the outcome (page 17) turns out to be mostly rarity of the attempt.
What evidence would actually settle this? Not more observational data — more attempts. The dataset can motivate but not adjudicate. A real test looks like: athletes who pre-declare a pacing plan (so intent isn't inferred after the fact), tracked across seasons as they practice it — exactly the kind of longitudinal attempt log that today exists for a single athlete (65 self-recorded attempts, 62% success, ~15 years). The paper's ask is that more athletes and coaches generate this data.
Caveats. "Opened at/below final average" is a conservative attempt-detector: it misses a planned negative split abandoned in hour 2, and it is computed against the athlete's own final result, so a catastrophic collapse could technically qualify (none of the nine is one — see their splits). Detection requires lap data, so events with only a halfway checkpoint can't contribute attempts. The denominator is full-day efforts only; early stoppers are excluded by the same rule used across this site.
