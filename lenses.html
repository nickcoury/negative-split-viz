21 · Three lenses, and two of them lie

Any claim that pacing causes outcomes in observational race data has to survive one objection: split % and final distance are mechanically coupled. A collapse produces a huge positive split AND a short race, whatever the plan was — so "even pacing goes with big days" is partly a restatement of "not collapsing goes with big days." This page takes the objection seriously. It shows the two analyses a motivated reader could produce — one "proving" even pacing wins, one "proving" banking early wins — shows why each is confounded in the opposite direction, and then builds the cleaner comparison both sides should accept: matched pairs inside the same race.

LENS A Split vs outcome — flattering, and coupled

Mean final distance by split band, all classified 24h halves. Reads as "pace even, run far." The catch: a blow-up lands you in the right-hand bins and shortens your race — the x-axis partly contains the y-axis. This lens overstates the case FOR even pacing.

LENS B Opening pace vs outcome — the skeptic's chart, equally coupled

Outcome relative to the athlete's other career best, against how fast they opened relative to that same reference (predictor uses no data from this race's outcome — looks clean!). It reads as "the harder you open, the better": the 1.3×+ openers beat their old best 76% of the time. The catch runs the other way: careers span years, and an athlete opens fast on the days they've gotten fitter — opening pace is a fitness thermometer, not a strategy dial. This lens overstates the case AGAINST holding back. Anyone quoting a curve like this at you (in either direction) hasn't solved the problem, just picked a side of it.

LENS C Matched at halfway — same race, same fitness, different shape

Take two athletes in the same event who reach 12 hours within 1% of the same distance — same day, same course, same weather, and (by the halfway evidence) closely matched day-fitness. One arrived evenly; the other was already slowing between hour 6 and 12. Then watch the second half. Reading it honestly

The matched comparison is the strongest observational evidence this dataset can produce, and it favors the even arrival — but it is still not causation. "Already slowing at halfway" can mean "chose to open too fast" (a pacing decision) or "body already failing" (which no pacing plan fixes). The design removes day-quality, course and conditions; it cannot remove that last ambiguity. What it does establish: two athletes at the same halfway mark are not in the same race — the shape of the first half carries ~7 km of information about the second. The halfway split, the number every 24-hour broadcast quotes, is not a sufficient statistic.
What would settle it is assignment, not observation: athletes pre-committing to opening paces (or being assigned them across training races), with the plan recorded before the gun. That experiment has effectively never been run at 24 hours by anyone except one athlete logging his own attempts. Until it is run at scale, both "negative splits are optimal" and "banking is optimal" are beliefs. The data's honest contribution is narrower and still important: the fade is enormous, evenness tracks success everywhere we can measure it, the strategy is untested, and the cost of testing it appears low.
Caveats. Pairs share athletes and events, so the 7,340 pairs are not 7,340 independent observations — which is why the per-event chart is shown: 43 events with ≥10 decided pairs, mean win rate 64%, exactly one below 50%. Arrival shape is measured as second-quarter vs first-quarter distance (Q2/Q1), which uses no second-half information. Sampled (hourly) feeds are included; their Q2/Q1 is coarser but unbiased.
