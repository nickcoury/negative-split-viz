18 · Your best day is your most even

The strongest evidence in this dataset that even pacing and peak performance travel together comes from comparing athletes with themselves. Cross-sectional comparisons (top-3 pace more evenly than the rest) can always be waved off as "better runners are different people." A career view can't: same engine, same athlete, different day, different pacing. This page looks at every athlete with three or more classified 24-hour races and asks — when they ran their biggest distance, how did they split that day?

Chart 18.1 — Where a race lands in your career, by how you split it

Every race by every ≥3-race athlete, binned by split. y = where that race ranks in the athlete's own career (1.0 = their best distance ever, 0 = their worst). The staircase is the finding: races split under +5% land around the 75th percentile of that athlete's own career; races split over +30% are their personal worsts. The far-left bar (negative) ranks about level with the even bins — but read it with care: it holds 18 races, and essentially none of them (see Exploration 20) come from athletes who had practiced negative splitting. The staircase tells you the fade costs; it cannot tell you what a practiced negative split would return.

Chart 18.2 — 590 careers, one number each

Per-athlete Spearman correlation between final distance and split % across their own races. Negative = their longer races are their more even races. If pacing had nothing to do with outcome this would pile up around zero; instead nearly nine in ten careers sit left of it. (The spikes at −1 and −0.5 are quantization, not artefact: a 3-race career can only produce a handful of ρ values — −1 there means the ordering is perfect: best distance, most even; worst distance, least even.)

Chart 18.3 — Career-best day vs the rest, athlete by athlete

The 25 biggest careers in the dataset (by best distance). For each: the split on their career-best day (●, colored by band) against the mean split of their other races (○ gray). The arrow almost always points the same way — toward even. Hover for the athlete's races.

Chart 18.4 — The two distributions

Split distribution of career-best days vs all other days, same athletes. Best days cluster lower and tighter; other days smear far right. Note that best days are still mostly positive splits — the realized optimum is a controlled fade, and the argument is about its size. Medians marked on the chart.

Reading it honestly

Within a career, evenness and peak performance are almost inseparable. of ≥3-race athletes show the negative correlation, career-best days are on average points more even than the same athlete's other days, and for of athletes the career-best is also the career-most-even outright.
What this can and cannot say about even vs negative. What it says clearly: the fade is what loses 24-hour races — every percentage point surrendered past ~+5% costs career percentile, monotonically, all the way down the staircase. What it cannot adjudicate is whether a negative split beats an even one, because the comparison barely exists in nature: the realized best days (Sorokin's 319.6 km included) sit at +2 to +10%, and the negative bin is 18 races, almost none by athletes who had practiced the strategy. Basketball's shot charts once "showed" the three-pointer didn't pay — measured on players who never practiced it. Whether a trained negative split is a hidden gem or a dead end is precisely the open question this dataset can motivate but not answer; Exploration 23 holds the only attempt log in existence.
Causation caveat, stated plainly. Split % and final distance are mechanically coupled — a mid-race collapse produces both a huge positive split and a short distance, whatever the plan was. This chart cannot distinguish "even pacing produced the big day" from "the big day is what not blowing up looks like." What it does establish is the descriptive frontier: nobody's career best came with a big fade — 24-hour bests are run even-ish or not at all. Isolating the causal share would need within-athlete natural experiments (e.g., the same athlete's planned-conservative vs planned-aggressive starts), which is exactly what the intentionality metric begins to enable.
