23 · N = 1 — the attempt log

Exploration 20 established that the negative split is essentially untested in elite 24-hour racing. Essentially — because one athlete has been running the experiment on himself since 2011 and writing it down. This is Nick Coury's self-reported log of every race he started intending to negative split: 65 scored entries over 15 years, most on trail, many far below 24 hours in duration. It is everything a clinical dataset is not — self-scored, uncontrolled, terrain-confounded, N=1 — and it is also the only multi-year record of deliberate negative-split practice known to exist in ultrarunning. It is presented here as exactly that: not proof, but the shape of what practicing the skill looks like, and the template for the data the field should want from many athletes.

Chart 23.1 — Fifteen years of attempts

Every logged attempt: date vs achieved split, colored by race class. The green band is a negative split; note how the points tighten around zero as the practice accumulates — and where the three catastrophic outliers come from (a blown Achilles at two World 24s bracketing the American Record, and the 2024 Western States collapse). ◆ marks the fixed-time (24h/6h) attempts — the discipline where the skill lands last.

Chart 23.2 — The skill transfers up-distance last

Success rate (achieved split < 0) by race class. Below 100 miles the practiced skill succeeds at roughly a two-thirds rate — at 24 hours it has succeeded twice in six attempts, and both successes (Desert Solstice 2018 and the 2021 American Record) came a decade into the practice. "24 hours are hard" appears three times in the log's notes.

Chart 23.3 — What failure looks like when you were trying

The distribution of his 25 failed attempts (positive splits) against the elite 24-hour field's split distribution from this dataset. Even the failures cluster a few percent above even — intent alone compresses the fade by an order of magnitude, with three injury/blow-up exceptions. This is the log's most transferable observation, and it matches the census: among the nine elite attempts on record, the worst outcome was +6.5%.

Chart 23.4 — The DB-verified spine: his 24-hour arc

The subset of his career this project's database independently verifies from official timing (lap-resolved where available): 24-hour halves splits and distances, 2011 → 2023. Split falls +49% → −1.03% as distance climbs 101 → 173 miles; the 2023 Worlds spike is the Achilles, not the pacing. Hover for distances. The log itself

Verbatim from the source sheet (self-reported). Rows marked ⊂ are splits inside another row's race and are included in the owner's own tallies. The notes column is the point: terrain asymmetries, injuries, and "went out too fast" are recorded against the failures — the raw material of an attempt log.

Reading it honestly

This is what the 3-point analogy predicts practice should look like. A contrived first attempt (2011), a long gap, then deliberate volume from 2017 on: success arrives first at 50k, then 50 miles, then 100k, reaches 100 miles by 2020–21, and lands at 24 hours exactly twice — with the American Record among them. The log neither proves the strategy is optimal nor that it generalizes beyond one athlete. It proves the skill is trainable, that its failure mode under intent is mild, and that its acquisition follows the years-long trajectory Nick's hypothesis predicts. One athlete's N=65 is an anecdote; forty athletes keeping this log would be a literature.
Caveats, all of them. Self-reported and self-scored; halves on trail courses are affected by terrain asymmetry (several notes say "second half harder/easier" — both directions appear); distances range 40k–100mi plus fixed-time; successes and failures are the athlete's own accounting (40/25, reproduced independently by this page's build from the sheet's rows); and the author of the log owns this research project. None of that is hidden — it is the reason this page exists separately from the database, which contains none of these rows except the officially-timed 24-hour races in chart 23.4.
