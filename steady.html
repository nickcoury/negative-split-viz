Exploration 33 · Seconds off a straight line

A second, independent definition of smoothness, and a different bet from Exploration 32's. Draw a trend line through the performance. Measure the area between the actual pace and that line, counting what is above and what is below equally. Put it in seconds.

wobble = ∫₀¹ | pace(u) − trend(u) | du  ·  seconds per mile
The series is seconds per mile ahead of your own average pace, so up is faster — the same convention intent_s uses, and the trend's slope is positive for a race that sped up. The number that comes out is a mean as well as an integral, because [0, 1] has width one: on average, this many seconds per mile away from your own straight line.

Three deliberate differences from Exploration 32's rough. (1) A straight line, not a line plus a bend. That is the substantive claim: a race that curves is not smooth, it is merely curved, and only a steady drift in one direction should count as steady. The quadratic and cubic fits are computed too, so the claim can be tested rather than assumed — chart 33.5. (2) An L1 area, not an RMS. Squaring lets one walked lap dominate a whole 24-hour race; the absolute value does not. (3) Seconds, not percent. Magnitude counts, exactly as it does for intentionality — which also means the two are in the same unit and can be put on one scatter (33.4).

The hypothesis this page exists to test — and there are two questions inside it. First: does the NUMBER survive a coarser feed? It does not. validate.py:steadiness_units pins that an L1 integral gives the same area on a smooth curve at 20 points and at 400 — but real pace is not smooth, and resampling 4,004 lap-complete ultras down to a marathon's mats makes the native-resolution number read 0.52× what the same runner scored at full resolution, indistinguishable from the naive standard deviation this whole family was meant to avoid. On a fixed grid of equal blocks the same integral holds far better — 0.88× at ten checkpoints, 0.99× at fifty. The norm was never the problem; the mesh was. Chart 33.2 is that measurement.
Second, and it is the question that decides whether any of that matters: is the shift a WASH, or is it systematic? A coarse feed that made some races look smoother and some rougher would cost accuracy on a single race and cost a conclusion nothing. Chart 33.3 asks it four ways, and the answer splits cleanly: the levels are strongly biased, the findings are not. Essentially every race gets smoother when coarsened — it is a one-directional filter, not a wash — so a level is never comparable across cadences. But the shift is close to a pure rescaling: it barely depends on how rough the race was, it preserves rank correlation 0.84 at native resolution and 0.97 on a fixed grid, and the headline relationship this metric exists to state — its correlation with seconds of intentionality — reads 0.71 at full resolution against 0.69 at a marathon's cadence. So: use the finest feed you have and let it buy accuracy, compare levels only within one cadence, and a rank-based finding carries across them.

33.5 — Is the straight line the right trend?

The substantive claim, tested. Each rung's wobble against a line, against a line plus one bend, and against a cubic — drawn as a fraction of the straight-line value. A rung whose bars barely fall is one where the deviation from a line is genuine unsteadiness; a rung where they collapse is one where much of what the straight line charges for is a single smooth curve, and the choice of trend is doing the work rather than the running.

Why the x axis defaults to a percentile. The selection rule is not the same on every rung: eight of them take everything inside 110% of the world record, so on the raw ratio their entire population is squeezed into 1.00–1.10 and overplots into one blob, while the 100-mile and 24-hour rungs — selected by an absolute bar instead — spread from 1.1 to 1.6. Ranking each race within its own rung puts them all on one readable axis and asks the same question. Switch to the raw ratio when a single distance is selected above.

Where this definition is still soft

A coarse feed has a floor this metric cannot go below, and it runs the OPPOSITE way to the smoothing loss. A piecewise-constant measurement of a perfectly steady drift is not on the line — inside a block the line moves while the measurement does not — so a genuinely perfect race scores rise / 4n rather than zero. On a lap list that is nothing (a 24-hour race with 180 laps and a 200 s/mi drift floors at 0.28 against a typical 26). On a marathon's ten mats with a 30 s/mi drift it is 0.75 s/mi against a typical 3.4 — a fifth of the number. So a coarse feed loses real wobble and gains arithmetic, which is why the measured ratios in 33.2 are the thing to quote and not either effect on its own. Pinned by validate.py:steadiness_units.

Seconds are not scale-free, and that was the point. A 24-hour runner averaging 604 s/mi has more seconds available to lose than a miler averaging 241, so part of the ladder in 33.6 is base pace rather than steadiness. The % of own pace unit removes that and is the fairer cross-rung axis; the seconds axis is the one a runner can act on. Neither is the right answer alone.

The trend is fitted to the race it is judging. Every performance is measured against its own best-fit line, so this asks "was the drift steady", never "was the drift right". A runner who fades enormously but perfectly linearly scores as steady — correctly, under this definition, and that is worth knowing when reading 33.8.

And a stop is a spike, not a stop. An L1 norm deliberately refuses to let one catastrophic block dominate, which is the right call for measuring steadiness and the wrong one for finding stops. The census's stop_recover verdict reads the raw gaps between crossings and is the tool for that.
