Exploration 33 · Seconds off a straight line

A second, independent definition of smoothness, and a different bet from Exploration 32's. Draw a trend line through the performance. Measure the area between the actual pace and that line, counting what is above and what is below equally. Put it in seconds.

wobble = ∫₀¹ | pace(u) − trend(u) | du  ·  seconds per mile
The series is seconds per mile ahead of your own average pace, so up is faster — the same convention intent_s uses, and the trend's slope is positive for a race that sped up. The number that comes out is a mean as well as an integral, because [0, 1] has width one: on average, this many seconds per mile away from your own straight line.

Three deliberate differences from Exploration 32's rough. (1) A straight line, not a line plus a bend. That is the substantive claim: a race that curves is not smooth, it is merely curved, and only a steady drift in one direction should count as steady. The quadratic and cubic fits are computed too, so the claim can be tested rather than assumed — chart 33.4. (2) An L1 area, not an RMS. Squaring lets one walked lap dominate a whole 24-hour race; the absolute value does not. (3) Seconds, not percent. Magnitude counts, exactly as it does for intentionality — which also means the two are in the same unit and can be put on one scatter (33.3).

The hypothesis this page exists to test, and the answer. The argument for this form was that it should be resistant to sampling: more checkpoints buy a more accurate estimate of the same integral rather than a bigger number, and fewer give a worse estimate of the same quantity. That is exactly true of a smooth signal — an L1 integral converges as the mesh refines, and validate.py:steadiness_units pins that arithmetic on constructed curves, identical at 20 points and at 400. It is false of real running. Resample 4,004 lap-complete ultras down to a marathon's mats and the native-resolution number reads 0.52× what the same runner scored at full resolution — statistically indistinguishable from the naive standard deviation this whole family was designed to avoid. Pace has genuine structure at every scale a lap list can see, and integrating over the athlete's own checkpoints inherits it.
The fix is not the norm, it is the mesh. Evaluate the same integral on a fixed grid of equal-distance blocks and it holds: 0.88× at ten checkpoints and 0.99× at fifty. So the metric ships at four resolutions, the controls below switch between them, and chart 33.2 is the measurement. Use native to compare performances timed the same way; use a fixed grid to compare across rungs.

33.1 — One race, and the area being measured

The pace series against its own fitted trend, with the deviation shaded — that shaded area, divided by the length of the race, is the number. Below the trend and above it are shaded differently but count identically; that is the definition. A ranking across rungs is partly a ranking of their timing. Even on a fixed grid, a rung with fewer native marks than the grid has blocks is interpolated into it and reads low — so the steadiest performances in the whole file will be the coarsely-timed ones. 33.4 — Is the straight line the right trend?

The substantive claim, tested. Each rung's wobble against a line, against a line plus one bend, and against a cubic — drawn as a fraction of the straight-line value. A rung whose bars barely fall is one where the deviation from a line is genuine unsteadiness; a rung where they collapse is one where much of what the straight line charges for is a single smooth curve, and the choice of trend is doing the work rather than the running.

33.5 — Across the ladder

Median wobble per rung, shortest first, at the chosen resolution and unit. In seconds per mile this carries the base-pace confound — a 4:00/mi miler and an 8:30/mi 24-hour runner are not on one scale — so switch the unit control to % of own pace for the scale-free version and read both.

Where this definition is still soft

A coarse feed has a floor this metric cannot go below, and it runs the OPPOSITE way to the smoothing loss. A piecewise-constant measurement of a perfectly steady drift is not on the line — inside a block the line moves while the measurement does not — so a genuinely perfect race scores rise / 4n rather than zero. On a lap list that is nothing (a 24-hour race with 180 laps and a 200 s/mi drift floors at 0.28 against a typical 26). On a marathon's ten mats with a 30 s/mi drift it is 0.75 s/mi against a typical 3.4 — a fifth of the number. So a coarse feed loses real wobble and gains arithmetic, which is why the measured ratios in 33.2 are the thing to quote and not either effect on its own. Pinned by validate.py:steadiness_units.

Seconds are not scale-free, and that was the point. A 24-hour runner averaging 604 s/mi has more seconds available to lose than a miler averaging 241, so part of the ladder in 33.5 is base pace rather than steadiness. The % of own pace unit removes that and is the fairer cross-rung axis; the seconds axis is the one a runner can act on. Neither is the right answer alone.

The trend is fitted to the race it is judging. Every performance is measured against its own best-fit line, so this asks "was the drift steady", never "was the drift right". A runner who fades enormously but perfectly linearly scores as steady — correctly, under this definition, and that is worth knowing when reading 33.7.

And a stop is a spike, not a stop. An L1 norm deliberately refuses to let one catastrophic block dominate, which is the right call for measuring steadiness and the wrong one for finding stops. The census's stop_recover verdict reads the raw gaps between crossings and is the tool for that.
