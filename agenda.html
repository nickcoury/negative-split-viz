25 · The experiment nobody has run

Everything on this site reduces to five statements, each with its own page of evidence and caveats: the elite 24-hour fade is enormous and out of line with every shorter distance; the negative splits that exist are mostly accidents; evenness tracks success everywhere we can measure it, though observation cannot prove causation; the fade is discrete collapse, foreshadowed hours ahead, not smooth physiological destiny; and the strategy that attacks it is essentially untested — nine attempts in 3,264 efforts, none catastrophic, one deliberate practitioner whose logs show the skill is trainable and physiologically real. This page is the conclusion that follows: not a verdict, but a protocol. What would it take for the sport to actually answer the question?

Ask 1 — Athletes & coaches: log attempts, not just outcomes

The single highest-value, lowest-cost action. An attempt log turns an anecdote into a data point: declare the plan before the gun, score the outcome after, keep the failures. One athlete's 65-row log is the only one known to exist; forty would constitute a literature. The template — the same columns that made Exploration 23 possible, plus the one thing that log lacks (the pre-declared opening pace):

FieldWhy it matters
Date · race · distance/durationcontext, and the up-distance transfer question
Planned first-half pace/distancedeclared before the start — this is what makes intent measurable instead of inferred
Actual halves (time or distance)the outcome, in the same units every page here uses
Terrain/conditions asymmetrythe honest confound in trail halves — record it, don't hide it
Notes: what broke, whenfailure attribution — injury vs pacing error vs conditions — is where the learning lives

Ask 2 — Race organizers & timers: publish the laps

Every verdict on this site required lap-level data, and 27 negative/even splitters still cannot be scored because only a halfway checkpoint survives. Full lap exports — which the timing systems already record — are the difference between a rarity statistic and an explanation. The 50-mile split at a 100-mile race costs nothing to publish and is currently thrown away almost everywhere.

Ask 3 — The near-term study anyone could run

A season-long, pre-registered cohort: recruit 20–40 experienced ultrarunners, have each declare opening-pace plans for their B-races (some conservative of goal pace, some at it), log all of them, and compare outcomes within athlete across the season. No lab, no randomization ethics problem (athletes choose their races; plans rotate), power that this observational dataset can never reach — recall that its entire attempt arm is nine runs. The N=1 log suggests the effect of intent alone is large (failed attempts cluster near even rather than at the +14% field median), which is exactly what makes a small cohort worth running.

Ask 4 — The long game: treat it like the three-point shot

The basketball analogy is not decoration; it is the null hypothesis this project proposes. A strategy can be genuinely superior and still look bad in every historical dataset, if executing it requires a skill nobody has spent years building. The attempt log shows the skill takes years and transfers up-distance last; the physiology shows what it looks like when built. The question "is a trained negative split optimal at 24 hours?" is unanswerable today by design of the sample, not by nature of the answer — and that is the strongest reason to generate the sample.

What this project will claim in the paper — no more, no less. (1) Negative and even splits are ~1% of elite 24-hour performances, and mostly accidental — quantified for the first time against a full-field denominator. (2) Pacing evenness and performance are tightly associated at every level of analysis, including within careers. (3) The association cannot currently be read causally — and the biggest single reason is that the deliberate strategy has effectively never been attempted. (4) The physiological and behavioral structure of the fade (collapse, foreshadowed, with stops) is consistent with a trainable skill gap, not an immutable limit. (5) Therefore: a research and coaching agenda, per the asks above. Anyone who wants to prove this hypothesis wrong should also want these data to exist.
