28 · Twice — the repeat negative splitters

Exploration 17 counted negative splits. This page counts the people. If a negative split at this distance is mostly an accident — a blow-up that happened to close, or a two-hour stop that sank the mid-race average — then it should scatter across the field like a coin landing on its edge, and almost nobody should own two. If instead it is a repeatable skill, it should concentrate in the same names. That is a testable difference, and the dataset is now big enough to test it: … classified races by … athletes, … of whom appear more than once.

The unit here is a RACE, not a performance. One 24-hour race yields up to two classified efforts — the whole day (halves) and the embedded 100 miles inside it (100mi_within_24h) — and counting those separately lets a single race make somebody a "repeat" splitter on its own. Ari Lindsten's 2012 Tapiola is negative on both; that is one negative split, not two. Everything below counts distinct races, and a race counts as negative if any method on it is. The per-effort detail is kept and shown, never summed.

The roster — click a row for the race-by-race detail

Sorted by negative races, then by median split. Whole-day counts only races where the full 24 hours ran negative — the strictest reading of the question, and the reason the top of this table is much shorter than the bottom. Effort type counts the negative/even efforts, not races, so an athlete with one even full day and three even embedded hundreds shows four — those are four different things and one race count hides it. Deliberate is intentionality read as a yes/no (verdict held back or metronome) over the efforts we can judge; Intentionality is the same thing read continuously, in s/mi, as their median and their most conservative single effort — an athlete can be 2/2 deliberate at −1.1 s/mi and another at −11.7, and those are not the same act. Both are blank where there is no lap data to judge on, which is not the same as a "no". Career %ile is where the negative/even races sit among that athlete's own 24-hour distances (100 = their best ever). Aliases lists the database rows that had to be merged to see the person at all. Inside an expanded row, verdicts read not profiled where a race has laps but is too far from even to be in the profiled set, and no lap data where the source only ever gave a halfway checkpoint.

Is it luck?

Nineteen repeat splitters out of four thousand athletes could easily be what randomness looks like — plenty of people race ten times, and a 2% event repeats by accident. So the count has to be compared against a null that is genuinely hard to beat.

Chart 28.2 — The permutation null

A restricted permutation: the number of negative/even races inside each group is held fixed and only their owners are reshuffled, 20,000 times. That means any group-level rate difference survives into the null untouched — if DUV's 2-checkpoint halves are biased negative, or if championship fields pace more evenly, the null gets exactly the same excess and the test is not fooled by it. The only thing being asked is whether the labels land on the same people. Grouped by event holds each race's field fixed; by event × finishing quartile also holds fixed this project's own finding that stronger finishers pace more evenly, so a repeat splitter cannot score merely by being good. Bars are the null distribution; the vertical line is what actually happened.

How they did it — the intentionality read

Rarity says nothing about intent. Exploration 16's metric — seconds of intentionality, the mean gap in s/mi between an athlete's foresight line and the pace they finally averaged — separates the two mechanisms: negative means they held back (never ran ahead of what they could sustain), positive means they went out ahead and the faster second half fell out of the fade. Combined with the longest gap between timing crossings, that gives every lap-resolved effort one of the census's four verdicts. This is available only where lap data exists — … of the repeat splitters' negative/even races have it.

Roster vs census — like for like

The roster's efforts skew toward the embedded hundred, which the census already shows is the more deliberate of the two settings — so the headline mix above cannot be compared against the census's directly. Split by effort, and against the athletes who did it once rather than against a census that contains these same efforts, it can. Both pairs move the same way: a negative/even split that belongs to a repeat splitter is more likely to have been planned, and on the whole day the difference is large.

The same thing again, by effort type

"The embedded 100 miles" is still two different acts, and the split between them is what the athlete did next. 100-in-24 is the first hundred of a full day, paced on purpose and then carried another twelve hours. 100 & stop is a 100-mile race that happened to begin inside a 24-hour event — the athlete stopped at the mark, so holding back over it is the ordinary road-racing skill, not the thing this paper is about. 100+ & stop ran past a hundred and then stopped short of the horn. Splitting them apart is the granular view: the 24h row is the only one that describes the full-day problem, and it is the row where the roster and the one-timers differ most.

Chart 28.4 — Does the same person do it the same way twice?

Each marker is one lap-resolved negative/even effort by a repeat splitter, coloured by verdict; the grey line joins one athlete's efforts in chronological order. x = seconds of intentionality (left of zero = held back on purpose), y = split %. A short line means a consistent mechanism; a line that crosses the zero rule means the same athlete produced one planned even race and one accidental one — which is the difference between a skill and a coincidence, drawn per person. ◆ marks a detected stop.

Chart 28.5 — Are the even races their good races?

For each repeat splitter with both kinds of race, the mean 24-hour distance of their negative/even races against the mean of their other races. This is the careers design narrowed to the people who can actually be asked: the athlete is their own control, so ability, era and talent drop out. It is still not causal — a good day produces both a long race and an even one — but the direction is worth seeing on the repeat set specifically.

Two ways this could be an artefact

Chart 28.6 — Evidence quality, race by race

Not every negative split is equally well measured. A race with two checkpoints is a DUV 12-hour split, snapped to the last complete lap, which biases split_pct more negative than the truth by roughly loop/total (see docs/methodology.md) — and on such a race the embedded-100 figure is worse still, being interpolated from the same two points. A race with 10+ checkpoints is lap-derived and precise. If the repeat splitters lived mostly in the 2-checkpoint tier, the finding would be a measurement artefact. Bars: the negative/even races of the roster, by checkpoint count.

Are they simply the dataset's most consistent pacers?

If a repeat splitter's whole career sat in a narrow band that happens to straddle zero, "twice" would be arithmetic rather than skill. Caveats. Identity is the measurement here. The database keys athletes on (name, country) and the country half fragments people — the same runner arrives as USA from one timer, US from another and NULL from a legacy split file — so this page re-keys on name tokens alone and accepts the mirror-image risk that two different people sharing a name merge into one. Every merged cluster and its aliases is listed in the roster table for inspection, and a validator pins the list. Coverage is not uniform: an athlete is only visible here if their races are in the dataset, so someone with two negative splits at an unarchived venue is simply absent, and the count is a floor. Verdicts exist only where laps exist, and lap-resolved events skew toward venues whose timing survived. The permutation controls for event and for within-event finishing quartile, but not for everything — an athlete who races the same lap-complete venue every year is measured more precisely there than elsewhere, and precision itself is not randomised. Finally, the repeat set is small: 19 people and 4 with two negatives. The clustering is unlikely to be luck; the mechanism behind it is still an inference, and Exploration 25 is what would settle it.
