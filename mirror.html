19 · The marathon mirror

The premise of this project is a contrast: in the marathon, the negative split is coached as the ideal; in 24-hour racing it is nearly extinct in practice. This page pins down the marathon half of that sentence with external, verifiable numbers — world-record splits and the published literature — so the contrast the rest of the site measures isn't resting on folklore.

What the running literature actually says

Negative splitting is doctrine, and almost nobody does it. Large-scale analyses of big-city marathons find only 1–8% of recreational finishers achieve a true negative split, with mean splits around +8%; roughly 87% positive-split. Faster runners pace more evenly: Boston-qualifying runners slow ~6–8% through late segments vs 11–15% for the rest — the same monotonic gradient this dataset shows for 24-hour finishing bands (top-3 ≈ +9.8% → rest ≈ +18.4%). A 2025 review argues negative splits reflect superior thermoregulatory and glycogen management — i.e. a skill, not a lottery ticket.

The mirror, stated carefully. In the marathon, the ideal is even-to-negative, elites achieve within ±1–2%, and the debate is about the last percent. In 24-hour racing, the elite median is +14%, the best performances ever run sit at +2 to +10%, and a genuine negative split is attempted (see Exploration 20) by ~0.3% of elite efforts. Either 24-hour racing is physiologically exempt from the pacing principles every shorter distance obeys — or there is a large, unexplored margin sitting on the table. Nobody currently has the data to say which. That is the research gap this project documents.
Caveats. Marathon records are self-selected showcase performances on paced, flat courses; 24-hour racing involves eating, stops, and circadian lows that make some fade physiologic rather than tactical (see Exploration 22 for how much). The comparison establishes the assumption gap between the sports, not physiological equivalence.
